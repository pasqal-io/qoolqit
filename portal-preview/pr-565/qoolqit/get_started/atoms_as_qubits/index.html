Atoms as qubits

On this page, you will learn about:

  • How a neutral atom encodes a qubit and how a laser manipulates it
  • The Rydberg blockade: why two atoms close together cannot both be excited
  • How the distance between atoms and the drive amplitude decide whether a pair is blockaded

Computing with atoms

Section titled “Computing with atoms”

A neutral-atom quantum processor holds individual atoms in place with optical tweezers, at positions chosen by the user. Each atom is a qubit. Its electronic ground state ∣g⟩|g\rangle∣g⟩ encodes ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, and a highly excited Rydberg state ∣r⟩|r\rangle∣r⟩ encodes ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩:

∣0⟩≡∣g⟩,∣1⟩≡∣r⟩. |0\rangle \equiv |g\rangle, \qquad |1\rangle \equiv |r\rangle. ∣0⟩≡∣g⟩,∣1⟩≡∣r⟩.

What makes neutral atoms useful for computing is how they interact: through the Rydberg blockade, an excited atom stops its close neighbours from being excited. This is the building block of quantum computing with neutral atoms, and the one Pasqal's processors are built on.

Throughout this page, quantities are written in QoolQit's dimensionless units, in which no two atoms are closer than r~=1\tilde{r} = 1r~=1. The next page, Programming a neutral-atom QPU, explains where these units come from.

A laser couples the two levels. Its drive amplitude Ω~\tilde{\Omega}Ω~ sets how fast an atom is transferred between ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩. An isolated atom under a laser tuned to its transition oscillates between the two states, a Rabi oscillation: starting in ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, its probability of being in the Rydberg state at time t~\tilde{t}t~ is

P1(t~)=sin⁡2 ⁣(Ω~ t~2). P_1(\tilde{t}) = \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\tilde{\Omega}\,\tilde{t}}{2}\right). P1​(t~)=sin2(2Ω~t~​).

Blockaded or not: distance against drive

Section titled “Blockaded or not: distance against drive”

Two atoms that are both in the Rydberg state repel each other. The interaction energy decays quickly with their distance r~\tilde{r}r~.

Interaction energy

J~=1r~ 6 \tilde{J} = \frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{\,6}} J~=r~61​

Since r~≥1\tilde{r} \geq 1r~≥1, the interaction never exceeds J~=1\tilde{J} = 1J~=1.

The laser is tuned to excite an isolated atom. Once one atom is excited, its interaction shifts the transition of its neighbour, and the laser can only excite that one too if the shift is small compared with the drive amplitude.

  • If J~≫Ω~\tilde{J} \gg \tilde{\Omega}J~≫Ω~, the laser is too far out of tune to excite the second atom to the Rydberg state as well, and the pair holds at most one excitation. Because the laser drives both atoms equally, that excitation is shared: the pair oscillates between ∣00⟩|00\rangle∣00⟩ and the Bell state ∣Ψ+⟩=(∣01⟩+∣10⟩)/2|\Psi^+\rangle = (|01\rangle + |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}∣Ψ+⟩=(∣01⟩+∣10⟩)/2​ at the enhanced frequency 2 Ω~\sqrt{2}\,\tilde{\Omega}2​Ω~. This is the Rydberg blockade.
  • If J~≪Ω~\tilde{J} \ll \tilde{\Omega}J~≪Ω~, the shift is too small to matter: each atom oscillates on its own and ∣11⟩|11\rangle∣11⟩ is reached.

Setting J~=Ω~\tilde{J} = \tilde{\Omega}J~=Ω~ defines the distance that separates the two regimes.

Blockade radius

r~b=Ω~−1/6 \tilde{r}_b = \tilde{\Omega}^{-1/6} r~b​=Ω~−1/6

Two atoms closer than r~b\tilde{r}_br~b​ are blockaded: their double excitation is suppressed. A larger drive amplitude shrinks the blockade radius, a smaller one extends it.

Inside the blockade radius (J~≫Ω~\tilde{J} \gg \tilde{\Omega}J~≫Ω~, for instance r~=1\tilde{r} = 1r~=1 with Ω~=0.1\tilde{\Omega} = 0.1Ω~=0.1), the probability of exciting both atoms stays close to zero. The pair behaves as a single two-level system, ∣00⟩↔∣Ψ+⟩|00\rangle \leftrightarrow |\Psi^+\rangle∣00⟩↔∣Ψ+⟩: the probability of exactly one excitation reaches one and oscillates faster than a single atom would, by the factor 2\sqrt{2}2​.

Outside the blockade radius (J~≪Ω~\tilde{J} \ll \tilde{\Omega}J~≪Ω~, for instance r~=2.5\tilde{r} = 2.5r~=2.5 with Ω~=0.1\tilde{\Omega} = 0.1Ω~=0.1), the probability of exciting both atoms reaches one. The two atoms are independent qubits: their interaction is negligible and each oscillates on its own.

At the blockade radius (J~≈Ω~\tilde{J} \approx \tilde{\Omega}J~≈Ω~) the pair moves continuously from one regime to the other. The double excitation is only partially suppressed, and the populations no longer follow a clean oscillation: the peaks change height from one cycle to the next.

Note that the blockade radius can be crossed in two ways: moving the atoms or changing the drive amplitude. Either way, only the ratio J~/Ω~\tilde{J} / \tilde{\Omega}J~/Ω~ matters.

What this means for a program

Section titled “What this means for a program”

The blockade is the building block QoolQit programs rely on: it is how the interaction between atoms shows up, tying their excitations together, and it is set by geometry and drive.

The positions of the atoms in a register decide which pairs are blockaded for a given Ω~\tilde{\Omega}Ω~, and that is how a problem is mapped onto the hardware.

Continue with Programming a neutral-atom QPU for the units and the full Hamiltonian, see how atom positions are chosen in Embedding, or watch the blockade at work in Solving a MWIS, where blockaded pairs become the edges of a graph.
